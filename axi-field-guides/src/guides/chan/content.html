AXI Channels & Handshake Field Guide

Everything in AXI rides on five one-way channels and one rule for moving a transfer across each of them. This guide covers why the channels are split the way they are, the VALID/READY handshake and the dependencies that keep it deadlock-free, clock and reset, the new credited transport in Issue L, register stages, and what changed from AXI3 and AXI4. It ends with cycle-level scenarios, verification checks and interview questions.

1 · Why AXI is built from channels

Nearly every rule in Chapter A2 serves one of four goals. Once you know which goal a rule serves, it is easy to remember.

Frequency

Any wire can be pipelined

Each channel goes one way and no channel is tied to another in time. That means a register stage can go on any channel without breaking the protocol. Long paths across a big SoC get register slices; short processor-to-memory paths stay direct.

Spec§A1.1, p.21 · §A2.5, p.40

Latency hiding

Ask early, answer late, answer out of order

Requests are separate from data, so a Manager can have many requests in flight while slow memory works on them. Only the start address is sent per burst. Transactions can complete out of order.

Spec§A1.1, p.21 · §A1.2, p.22

Bandwidth

Reads and writes never share a data path

Write data and read data have their own channels, each up to 1024 bits wide, with one strobe per byte on writes. A DMA can read and write at full rate at the same time. Request channels carry much less traffic than data channels, which is why interconnects often share them.

Spec§A1.1, p.21 · §A1.2, p.23 · §A1.2.1, p.24

Interoperability

One interface, everywhere

The same interface definition connects a Manager to an interconnect, a Subordinate to an interconnect, and a Manager straight to a Subordinate. To each side, an interconnect looks like just another component.

Spec§A1.2.1, p.24

Background: AXI arrived with AMBA 3 (AXI3) in 2003–2004 to replace the shared, pipelined AHB bus in high-performance SoCs. AXI4 (2010) widened bursts and removed write interleaving. AXI5 (Issue F, 2017) added optional features. Issue J (2023) dropped AXI3 and AXI4 from the document, and Issue L (2025) added credited transport. The revision history is on p.ii.

2 · Where it sits: five channels between two interfaces

A channel is a one-way connection from a transmitter (Tx) to a receiver (Rx). An interface is the set of channel ends on one component. A transfer is one cycle of communication on one channel; a transaction is all the transfers needed for one Manager–Subordinate exchange.

3 · Clock, reset and the two transports

One clock, one reset, and since Issue L a choice of two flow-control schemes. The choice is made per interface, not per channel.

ACLK
  • One clock per interface.
  • Inputs are sampled on the rising edge; outputs change only after it.
  • No combinational path from any input to any output on an interface.

Spec§A2.1.1, p.27

ARESETn
  • Single active-LOW reset.
  • May assert asynchronously; deasserts only on a rising ACLK edge.
  • Signals that must be LOW in reset stay LOW until at least the edge after ARESETn is HIGH. Everything else may take any value in reset.

Spec§A2.1.2 and Figure A2.1, p.27

Held LOW in reset
  • Every VALID (AW, W, AR from the Manager; B, R from the Subordinate).
  • On credited interfaces: every CRDT and CRDTSH. The transmitter starts with zero credits.
  • Not required: READY, payload, PENDING (PENDING may even stay HIGH).

5 · The VALID/READY handshake

The transmitter says "I have something" with VALID; the receiver says "I can take something" with READY. A transfer happens on a rising edge where both are HIGH. Three orderings are legal, plus one freedom that only the receiver has.

The transmitter's obligations

  • Must not wait for READY before asserting VALID. §A2.3, p.29
  • Once VALID is HIGH it stays HIGH until the handshake. §A2.3, p.29
  • The payload stays stable until the transfer. §A2.3, p.29
  • VALID is LOW in reset. §A2.3, p.29

The receiver's freedoms

  • May wait for VALID before asserting READY. §A2.3, p.29
  • May assert READY before VALID, and drop it again before VALID arrives. §A2.3, p.29
  • Default READY may be HIGH or LOW. On request channels HIGH is recommended for latency; then any valid request presented must be accepted. §A2.3, p.30
  • Still bound by §A2.1.1: no combinational path from VALID to READY on the same interface. §A2.1.1, p.27

Transaction-level relationships (§A2.6)

  • A write response always follows the last write transfer; read data always follows its request.
  • A Manager that issues a write must be able to supply all its data, and later accept its response, without depending on any other transaction.
  • A Manager that issues a read must be able to accept all its data the same way. Same-ID reads return in order, so storage is needed only per ID.
  • Either side may choose to handle one transaction at a time.
  • A Subordinate must not block data-less write requests because of transactions whose write data arrived early.
  • Nothing else links the channels: W may arrive before AW, or in the same cycle. An interconnect must realign them so data is shown only to the Subordinate it belongs to.
  • Writes without data (for example some cache maintenance writes) drop the W terms from the dependency rules.

7 · Credited transport (new in Issue L)

On a credited interface READY disappears. The receiver hands the transmitter credits, one per free buffer entry; the transmitter spends one per transfer. Every signal in both directions is registered, so nothing has to settle combinationally within one cycle.

1

Reset: the transmitter has no credits and the receiver has all of them. Every CRDT and CRDTSH is LOW. §A2.4.1, p.34

2

Give: each cycle a CRDT bit is HIGH gives one credit on that Resource Plane. A receiver gives at least 1 and at most 15 per plane, and at most 15 shared. §A2.4.1, p.34

3

Announce: PENDING is HIGH in the cycle before VALID, so a gated receiver can wake its clock. PENDING HIGH does not oblige VALID to follow. §A2.4.4, p.38

4

Spend: each cycle VALID is HIGH uses one credit; VALID is never HIGH with zero credits. There is no READY, so the transfer happens in that cycle. §A2.4.1, p.34

5

No same-cycle tricks: no combinational path between credit signals and anything else on the channel. A credit can't be spent in the cycle it arrives or given back in the cycle it's spent. §A2.4.1, p.34

Resource Planes

Independent lanes inside one channel

  • Each plane has its own credits. Transfers on different planes must not block each other between transmitter and receiver.
  • Num_RP_AWW (AW and W) and Num_RP_AR are each 1 to 8, default 1. B and R always have one plane.
  • RP is clog2(Num_RP) bits wide; with one plane there is no RP signal.
  • The AW and W of one transaction use the same plane.
  • No ordering across planes. So a Manager must not issue a request with the same ID as an outstanding transaction on the same channel but a different plane. It may interleave write data of different transactions if they are on different planes.
  • Interfaces may differ in plane count, but a Manager must not need more planes than its Subordinate provides.

Spec§A2.4.2 and Table A2.5, p.35

Shared credits

A pool any plane can draw on

  • Optional per channel (Shared_Credits_AW, _W, _AR); only with Credited transport and more than one plane.
  • CRDTSH gives one shared credit and may be asserted without CRDT. SHAREDCRD goes with VALID to say a shared credit was spent.
  • Planes stay independent whichever kind of credit is used.
  • Recommended: spend a dedicated credit when you have one, and keep shared ones for planes that have run out.
  • Mismatched settings still work (Table A2.7); tie unused SHAREDCRD inputs or CRDTSH inputs LOW.

8 · Why each rule exists, with real-time examples

Each card gives a rule, the reason behind it, an example with real numbers, and what goes wrong in a design that ignores it. Numbers assume a 128-bit data path at 1 GHz unless the card says otherwise.

Handshake§A2.3 p.29

VALID must not wait for READY

A transmitter may not hold VALID LOW until it sees READY.

Why

Receivers are explicitly allowed to wait for VALID before raising READY. If transmitters could also wait for READY, two compliant-looking blocks could each wait for the other forever. Putting the "go first" duty on one side, always the transmitter, makes every pairing safe.

Real-time example

A DDR controller's AR port raises ARREADY only after it sees ARVALID and checks its command queue, two cycles later. A DMA that waits for ARREADY first would never issue a read to it. The same DMA worked with an SRAM whose ARREADY was tied HIGH, which is why this bug often survives unit test.

If you break it

A hang with no error, and only with some partners. Formal tools and VIP flag it as a liveness or protocol violation.

Sources
  • Spec§A2.3, p.29
  • IllustrativeDDR and DMA timing
Handshake§A2.3 p.29

Once VALID, always VALID, until the transfer

After VALID rises it stays HIGH, with a stable payload, until the cycle VALID and READY are both HIGH.

Why

The receiver may already be acting on what it sees: allocating a buffer, starting a DRAM activate, decoding an address. If the transmitter could withdraw or change the request, the receiver would have to undo that work. Sticky VALID makes "the receiver saw it" and "it happened" the same thing.

Real-time example

An interconnect arbiter offers Manager A's request downstream. Two cycles later Manager B, with higher QoS, arrives. The arbiter may not swap B in while AWVALID is HIGH and AWREADY is LOW. It has to wait for A's handshake, or not offer A in the first place.

If you break it

A Subordinate that latched A's address but receives B's data, or two requests counted where one was sent. These are some of the most common interconnect bugs.

Sources
  • Spec§A2.3, p.29
  • Illustrativearbiter example
Handshake§A2.3 p.29–30

READY may come early, late, or drop

The receiver may wait for VALID, may raise READY first, and may lower READY again before VALID arrives. Request-channel READY is recommended HIGH by default.

Why

READY is the receiver's statement about its own capacity, which changes from cycle to cycle. Raising it early saves a cycle per transfer; that is why a default-HIGH AWREADY or ARREADY is recommended. With READY LOW by default every transfer takes at least two cycles. The price of default HIGH is that the receiver must be able to accept any valid request presented.

Real-time example

A Subordinate with a 4-entry request queue drives ARREADY = (entries free > 0), from a flop. When the queue is full ARREADY drops, even with no ARVALID; that is legal. Back-to-back reads move one per cycle while space lasts.

If you break it

A default-LOW READY halves the peak request rate. A default-HIGH READY with no room behind it drops a request: data loss, not a stall.

Sources
  • Spec§A2.3, p.29–30
  • SpecH.c §A3.2.2 Note, p.A3-42
  • Illustrativequeue design
Clock & reset§A2.1.1 p.27

No combinational path from input to output

On an interface, every output must come from a flop or from other outputs' logic, never straight through from an input.

Why

If both ends of an interface had combinational paths, for example READY = f(VALID) on one side and VALID = f(READY) on the other, the pair could form a loop with no flop in it. Even without a loop, input-to-output paths chain across components until timing fails. The rule makes every interface a clean timing boundary.

Real-time example

A peripheral computes PREADY-style AWREADY = AWVALID && !busy. On its own it looks harmless. Behind a Manager that also drives AWVALID from a combinational arbitration of its inputs, this path can run across three blocks and miss 1 GHz.

If you break it

Combinational loops in synthesis, or timing paths that fail only when two IPs are integrated. Lint tools that model AXI boundaries flag it.

Sources
  • Spec§A2.1.1, p.27
  • Backgroundtiming-closure reasoning
Clock & reset§A2.1.2 p.27

Assert reset any time, release it on an edge

ARESETn may assert asynchronously; deassertion must be synchronous to ACLK. Signals held LOW in reset may rise at the earliest on the edge after ARESETn is HIGH.

Why

Asynchronous assertion lets reset work even with no clock running. Synchronous release avoids flops leaving reset in different cycles (reset recovery and removal violations). The extra edge of margin covers skew in the reset tree, so the partner is guaranteed out of reset before the first VALID.

Real-time example

A 2-flop reset synchroniser in each IP releases reset on an ACLK edge. If the Manager's tree releases one cycle before the Subordinate's, a VALID driven on the very first edge could hit a Subordinate still in reset. Issue L made explicit that both sides wait for the edge after ARESETn is HIGH.

If you break it

The first transaction after power-on or a warm reset vanishes, or is half-accepted. It is intermittent because it depends on reset-tree skew.

Sources
  • Spec§A2.1.2, p.27
  • SpecTable B4.3 (Reset clarification), p.311
  • Backgroundreset synchroniser practice
Dependencies§A2.3.2.1 p.31

A write is answered only after its request and its last beat

BVALID waits for the AWVALID/AWREADY handshake and the WVALID/WREADY handshake of the WLAST beat.

Why

A write response tells the Manager the write is now the Subordinate's problem. From then on the Subordinate has to order it correctly against every later access to the same address. It can only do that hazard check once it knows the address. Waiting for WLAST makes sure the response covers all the data.

Real-time example

A CPU writes a flag, gets BRESP, then reads the flag through another path. If the Subordinate had answered before seeing AWADDR, it could not have known that read was to the same location, and the read could overtake the write.

If you break it

An AXI3-style Subordinate that answers before taking the address is not compliant with AXI4 or AXI5. It needs a wrapper that holds B until AW is accepted.

Sources
  • Spec§A2.3.2.1, p.31
  • SpecH.c §A3.3.1, p.A3-46 · §A3.3.2, p.A3-47
Dependencies§A2.3.2.1 p.31

AW and W are independent in both directions

The Manager must not wait for AWREADY or WREADY before raising AWVALID or WVALID. The Subordinate may wait for either VALID, or both, before raising either READY.

Why

Subordinates differ. One accepts the address first and streams data; another buffers data first and needs both to start. Letting the receiver choose, and forbidding the transmitter from choosing, covers both without a deadlock.

Real-time example

A width converter needs AWSIZE before it can pack W beats, so it waits for AWVALID before WREADY. An SRAM controller wants all data before the write, so it waits for WLAST before AWREADY. A Manager that drives both VALIDs freely works with both.

If you break it

See the Deadlock scenario in section 9: Manager waits for AWREADY, Subordinate waits for WVALID, and the bus stops.

Sources
  • Spec§A2.3.2.1, p.31
  • SpecH.c Caution, p.A3-46
  • Illustrativeconverter and SRAM examples
Dependencies§A2.3.2.2 p.32 · §A3.2.2 p.55

Read data only for a request that was accepted

RVALID waits for ARVALID and ARREADY. Even a Subordinate with a single data source raises RVALID only in response to a request.

Why

The Manager matches each R beat to an outstanding request by ID and counts beats to find the end. Unrequested data would have nothing to match. A FIFO-like peripheral that "always has data" must still wait to be asked.

Real-time example

A UART RX FIFO behind an AXI port has a byte ready. Driving RVALID because the FIFO is non-empty would hand a byte to a Manager that never asked, which would drop it or match it to the wrong ID.

If you break it

Lost data, or an R beat matched to a later request that then gets one beat too many.

Sources
  • Spec§A2.3.2.2, p.32
  • Spec§A3.2.2, p.55
Transactions§A2.6 p.41

A Manager must finish what it started, on its own

Having issued a write, a Manager must be able to supply all its data and then take its response. Having issued a read, it must be able to take all its data. None of this may depend on other transactions.

Why

Interconnects and Subordinates commit resources when they accept a request. If the Manager's ability to finish depended on some other transaction making progress, that other transaction could be stuck behind the resources this one holds: a cycle of waits across the system.

Real-time example

A DMA issues a 16-beat read and plans to empty its 4-beat buffer by writing to a second Subordinate. If that write is blocked behind the read in the interconnect, the read can't drain. The fix is to issue reads only up to buffer space, or to size the buffer for all outstanding read data.

If you break it

System-level deadlocks that appear only under particular traffic mixes, often found late in emulation.

Sources
  • Spec§A2.6, p.41
  • IllustrativeDMA example
Transactions§A2.6 p.41 · Table B4.2 p.309

Don't block data-less writes behind early data

A Subordinate must not stop accepting data-less write requests because some other write's data arrived before its request.

Why

Write data can lead its request. If a Subordinate refuses new AW requests while holding data whose AW has not come yet, and the next AW in line is a data-less write, the AW that would release the data may be queued behind it. Data-less writes have no W beats to unblock anything, so they must not be caught in that wait.

Real-time example

An interconnect forwards W for write X early, but X's AW sits behind a data-less cache maintenance write Y in the AW queue. A Subordinate that waits for X's AW before taking Y never gets X's AW.

If you break it

Deadlock in systems that mix normal writes with data-less write requests. Issue K made this rule explicit.

Sources

Transactions§A2.6 p.41

An interconnect must realign AW and W

When an interconnect routes by address, it must show write data as valid only to the Subordinate the data is for.

Why

W has no address. With W allowed ahead of AW, an interconnect that forwarded WVALID before decoding the matching AW could deliver data to the wrong port.

Real-time example

A 1×4 crossbar keeps a small FIFO of decoded AW destinations. W beats are steered by the head of that FIFO. If the FIFO is empty, W waits (WREADY LOW to the Manager), even though the Subordinates might be ready.

If you break it

Data written to the wrong Subordinate, or W beats of two writes mixed at one port.

Sources
  • Spec§A2.6, p.41
  • Backgroundcrossbar design
Register stages§A2.5 p.40

Any number of register stages, on any channel

VALID, READY and CRDT paths may each have any number of stages; channels may differ. VALID is delayed exactly like its payload (and RP, SHAREDCRD).

Why

No channel is tied to another in time, so delaying one channel never breaks a cross-channel rule. Keeping VALID aligned with its own payload is the one thing a stage must never break.

Real-time example

A GPU-to-memory path crosses 6 mm of die at 1.5 GHz and needs two slices on AR and R. W gets one, AW gets three because its fan-out is larger. Write data now often arrives before AW, which the protocol already allows.

If you break it

A stage that delays VALID but not the payload (or the reverse) makes the receiver sample the previous transfer's address or data.

Sources
  • Spec§A2.5, p.40
  • Illustrativefloorplan numbers
Register stages§A2.5 p.40

A full-rate slice needs two entries

To register READY as well as VALID without losing throughput, a slice has to hold two transfers.

Why

If READY to the upstream side comes from a flop, the slice only learns that downstream stalled one cycle after it already said "ready". It must have room for the transfer that arrives in that cycle. One entry for the transfer being presented plus one for the skid gives full throughput.

Real-time example

See the Register slice scenario: the Subordinate stalls in cycles 5–6, the slice holds two entries, and the Manager sees AWREADY drop only in cycles 6–7. Streaming runs at one per cycle with one extra cycle of latency.

If you break it

A one-entry slice with registered READY runs at 50%. A one-entry slice with combinational READY keeps a long timing path, which was the reason for adding the slice.

Sources
  • Spec§A2.5, p.40
  • Backgroundskid-buffer design
Credited transport§A2.2 p.28 · §A2.4.1 p.34

Credits replace READY

The receiver pre-grants buffer space as credits; a transfer happens whenever VALID is HIGH, and needs a credit.

Why

With READY, the transmitter has to see the receiver's state from the same cycle, either through a combinational path or through a skid buffer at every register stage. With credits, the transmitter already knows it has space. Every wire can be a pure pipeline flop and no skid buffers are needed along the way.

Real-time example

A 2 GHz mesh with three pipeline stages each way. With Valid-Ready each stage needs a 2-entry skid buffer per channel. With credits, the receiver's buffer is sized to the credit loop once and the stages are plain flops.

If you break it

Sending without a credit overflows the receiver's buffer: silent corruption, not backpressure.

Sources
  • Spec§A2.2, p.28 · §A2.4.1, p.34
  • Backgroundmesh pipelining
Credited transport§A2.4.1 p.34

1 to 15 credits per plane

A receiver gives at least one credit per Resource Plane and at most 15 per plane, plus at most 15 shared.

Why

At least one guarantees every plane can always make progress. The cap of 15 bounds the transmitter's counter at 4 bits per plane, so implementations agree without negotiating counter widths.

Real-time example

Credit loop: 1 cycle VALID flop + 2 cycles receiver turnaround + 1 cycle CRDT flop = 4 cycles, the same loop as Figure A2.7. Full rate needs ≥ 4 credits, well within 15. Add two register stages each way and the loop is 8.

If you break it

Too few credits: throughput capped at credits ÷ loop. A 16th credit overflows a 4-bit counter at the transmitter, making it believe it has 0.

Sources

Credited transport§A2.4.2 p.35

Same ID never spans two planes

A Manager must not issue a request with the same ID as an outstanding transaction on the same channel but on a different Resource Plane.

Why

Planes have no ordering between them; that is the point of planes. AXI's same-ID ordering promise can only hold if all same-ID traffic shares one plane.

Real-time example

A Manager puts posted writes on RP0 and latency-critical writes on RP1, all with AWID 4. RP1 traffic would overtake RP0, and B responses with ID 4 would come back in an order the Manager can't untangle.

If you break it

Responses matched to the wrong transaction. Give each plane its own IDs.

Sources
  • Spec§A2.4.2, p.35
  • Illustrativetraffic split
Credited transport§A2.4.4 p.38

PENDING: one cycle of warning

PENDING is HIGH in the cycle before VALID. When PENDING falls, VALID is LOW in the next cycle. PENDING may be HIGH with no transfer following.

Why

The receiver can gate its input clock while PENDING is LOW and be sure no transfer arrives unannounced. PENDING is only a hint upward, so a transmitter that can't predict its traffic simply ties it HIGH (the default).

Real-time example

A memory controller's AR input pipeline gates 40% of its idle cycles by watching ARPENDING. The ungating ICG needs one cycle, which is exactly the warning PENDING gives.

If you break it

VALID without PENDING the cycle before: the transfer lands in a gated flop and is lost. Register stages must keep PENDING one cycle ahead of VALID.

Sources
  • Spec§A2.4.4, p.38 · §A2.5, p.40
  • Illustrativegating numbers

12 · Verification

Build one channel-level agent per direction and compose it into Manager, Subordinate and passive (monitor) interface agents. Every timing choice the spec leaves free should be a random knob, because each one is a place where a partner can make a different choice than the one you tested against.

Agent knobs that find bugs

  • Per channel: VALID delay, READY delay, probability READY is HIGH before VALID, READY toggling while VALID is LOW.
  • Subordinate policy: wait for AWVALID only, WVALID only, or both before each READY.
  • W lead/lag versus AW: -16 to +16 beats.
  • Back-to-back with zero idle cycles, and long idles to check gating.
  • Credited mode: credit count per plane (1 to 15), CRDT return delay, shared-credit pool, PENDING tied HIGH vs exact.
  • 0 to N register stages between agent and DUT, per channel.

Backgroundagent architecture · knobs mirror §A2.3–A2.5

Assertions

  • Each VALID LOW in reset and on the first edge after ARESETn rises. §A2.1.2, p.27
  • VALID HIGH and READY LOW ⇒ VALID HIGH and payload unchanged next cycle. §A2.3, p.29
  • No X on VALID or READY out of reset; no X on payload while VALID. Background
  • BVALID only after an accepted AW and an accepted WLAST beat for that write. §A2.3.2.1, p.31
  • RVALID only with an outstanding accepted AR. §A2.3.2.2, p.32
  • WLAST exactly on beat AWLEN+1; RLAST exactly on beat ARLEN+1. §A3.2.1, p.53 · §A3.2.2, p.55
  • Credited: VALID only with credit > 0 on that plane (or a shared credit with SHAREDCRD); counters never exceed 15; CRDT LOW in reset. §A2.4.1, p.34
  • Credited: $rose(VALID) ⇒ $past(PENDING); !PENDING ⇒ ##1 !VALID. §A2.4.4, p.38
  • Credited: RP < Num_RP; AW and W of one write share an RP; no same-ID across planes while outstanding. §A2.4.2, p.35
  • Liveness: VALID HIGH ⇒ eventually READY (bounded in simulation). Glossary "in a timely manner", p.316

Scenarios that find real bugs

  • Whole W burst before AW, with the Subordinate also holding AWREADY LOW until WLAST. §A2.6, p.41
  • AW with no W for many cycles, while a data-less write follows. §A2.6, p.41
  • READY pulses while VALID is LOW; READY LOW for exactly one cycle in every burst. §A2.3, p.29
  • ARESETn asserted in the middle of bursts on every channel, released, traffic restarts. §A2.1.2, p.27
  • BREADY and RREADY held LOW for long periods with many transactions outstanding. §A2.6, p.41
  • Credited: exactly 1 credit per plane; shared credit only; 15 credits returned in a burst. §A2.4.1–A2.4.3, p.34–36
  • Credited: one plane's receiver stalled forever, other planes must keep moving. §A2.4.2, p.35

Functional coverage

  • Per channel: VALID-before-READY × READY-before-VALID × same-cycle; stall length bins.
  • W lead/lag bins vs AW; BREADY before vs after BVALID.
  • Outstanding depth per channel up to the DUT's limit.
  • Credited: credit count 0, 1, mid, max at each VALID; dedicated vs shared spend per plane; plane × channel.

13 · Test yourself

Questions in the style of a DV interview, from basic to advanced. Answer in your head first, then open the card.

Name the five AXI channels and say which side drives VALID on each. Basic

AW (write request), W (write data) and AR (read request): VALID from the Manager. B (write response) and R (read data and response): VALID from the Subordinate. READY always comes from the other side.

Spec§A1.2, p.22–23 · Table A2.2, p.30

When exactly does a transfer happen, and can the transmitter drop VALID if READY doesn't come? Basic

On a rising ACLK edge where VALID and READY are both HIGH. No: once VALID is asserted it stays asserted, with stable information, until that handshake.

Spec§A2.3, p.29

Can write data arrive at the Subordinate before the write request? Basic

Yes. The protocol defines no relationship between AW and W beyond the dependency rules, so W may come before AW or in the same cycle, for example when AW has more register stages. An interconnect must realign them before routing data.

Spec§A2.6, p.41

What is the recommended default for AWREADY and ARREADY, and what does it commit the Subordinate to? Basic

HIGH, to save a cycle per request. With it HIGH, the Subordinate must be able to accept any valid request presented. H.c adds that LOW forces every transfer to take at least two cycles.

Spec§A2.3, p.30 · H.c §A3.2.2, p.A3-42

Your Manager waits for AWREADY before asserting WVALID. It works with every Subordinate in your testbench. Is it compliant? Intermediate

No. The Manager must not wait for AWREADY or WREADY before asserting AWVALID or WVALID. A Subordinate is allowed to wait for WVALID before AWREADY; paired with such a Subordinate, your Manager deadlocks. Your testbench Subordinates simply never used that freedom. Add a Subordinate policy knob that does.

Spec§A2.3.2.1, p.31 · H.c Caution, p.A3-46

Why must BVALID wait for the AW handshake in AXI4 and AXI5 when AXI3 didn't require it? Intermediate

A write response hands responsibility for hazard checking against later transactions to the Subordinate, and it can't check hazards without the address. AXI3 didn't state the dependency, but no real component was expected to answer before taking the address. AXI4 made it a rule, so an AXI3 Subordinate that answered early needs a wrapper.

SpecH.c §A3.3.1, p.A3-46 · §A3.3.2, p.A3-47 · §A2.3.2.1, p.31

Can a Subordinate generate AWREADY combinationally from AWVALID? Intermediate

It may wait for AWVALID, but §A2.1.1 forbids combinational paths between inputs and outputs on an interface. So a READY that depends on VALID has to go through a flop and can rise at the earliest in the cycle after VALID. The alternative is to drive READY from internal state alone, for example HIGH while space is free. Many real IPs do have such paths and get away with it only until they meet a partner that does the same.

Spec§A2.1.1, p.27 · §A2.3, p.29

Why must a Subordinate keep accepting data-less write requests while it holds early write data? Intermediate

The AW that matches the early data may be queued behind the data-less write. If the Subordinate waits for that AW before taking the data-less one, neither ever arrives. Issue K added this as an explicit rule.

Spec§A2.6, p.41 · Table B4.2, p.309

On a credited channel, when can a credit given in cycle N first be used, and what are the limits on credits? Intermediate

In cycle N+1 at the earliest: no combinational path between credit signals and the rest of the channel. At least 1 and at most 15 credits per Resource Plane, plus up to 15 shared. In reset the transmitter has none and CRDT is LOW.

Spec§A2.4.1, p.34

What does PENDING promise, and what doesn't it promise? Intermediate

It promises that VALID is never HIGH without PENDING HIGH in the previous cycle, so a receiver may gate its clock while PENDING is LOW. It does not promise that a transfer follows: PENDING may be HIGH with no credit, may rise and fall with no transfer, and may be tied HIGH permanently, including in reset.

Spec§A2.4.4, p.38

Can a Manager use the same AWID on two Resource Planes? Can it interleave write data? Advanced

Not while a transaction with that ID is outstanding on the other plane: planes have no ordering between them. It may interleave write data of different transactions if they are on different planes. That is the only write-data interleaving AXI has allowed since AXI3's WID was removed in AXI4.

Spec§A2.4.2, p.35 · §A5.5, p.100

Your credited link has 3 VALID stages, 2 cycles of receiver turnaround and 3 CRDT stages, and the receiver has 6 buffer entries. What throughput do you get, and what would you change? Advanced

Loop = 3 + 2 + 3 = 8 cycles from spending a credit to being able to spend it again (Figure A2.7 is the same sum: 1 + 2 + 1 = 4). With 6 credits the channel carries at most 6 transfers per 8 cycles, 75%. For full rate the receiver needs at least 8 entries and credits (up to 15 are allowed), or fewer pipeline stages. Shared credits don't help a single plane's throughput; they only rebalance between planes.

SpecDerived from §A2.4.1, p.34 and §A2.4.3, p.36

Why does a register slice that registers READY need two entries to run at full rate? Advanced

With registered READY, the slice tells upstream "ready" based on last cycle's state. If downstream stalls this cycle, the transfer accepted this cycle still needs a place to go. One entry holds the transfer on offer downstream, one is the skid. With one entry you either lose every other cycle or need a combinational READY path, which is what the slice was meant to remove.

Spec§A2.5, p.40 · Backgroundskid-buffer design

Which signals must be LOW in reset, and which can be anything? Did that change in Issue L? Advanced

LOW: every VALID, and on credited interfaces every CRDT and CRDTSH. Anything: READY, payload, and PENDING (which may stay HIGH). H.c listed the VALIDs per side. Issue L states the rule for every signal that must be deasserted in reset, and clarifies that all of them wait until at least the rising edge after ARESETn is HIGH.
