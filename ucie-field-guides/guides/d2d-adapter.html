UCIe D2D Adapter Field Guide

The Die-to-Die Adapter sits between the protocol stacks (on FDI) and the Physical Layer (on RDI). It makes the link reliable (CRC and Retry), shares it between two protocol stacks, manages link states with the remote Adapter, and negotiates protocol and flit format at bring-up. This guide covers each of those jobs, the byte layout of every flit format, the rules and why they exist, flit-level scenarios, and how to verify it.

1 · Why the Adapter exists

The spec gives the Adapter four jobs. Every rule in Chapter 3 serves one of them.

Reliability

CRC on every flit, Retry when needed

Above 8 GT/s (Standard Package) or 12 GT/s (Advanced Package) the raw bit error rate is 1e-15 or worse, so bit errors will happen in normal operation. The Adapter adds a 16-bit CRC to every flit and, when negotiated, a Retry scheme based on PCIe Flit Mode so that errors are corrected without the Protocol Layer noticing.

Spec§3.0, p.58 · §3.7, p.92 · §3.8, p.93 · Tables 1-1 and 1-2, p.40

Sharing

Two protocol stacks, one link

A UCIe link can be faster than one protocol stack needs. The Adapter can multiplex two stacks over it, tagging each flit with a stack ID and keeping a separate link state machine for each stack.

Spec§3.1, p.59–60 · Figure 3-2, p.61

Link state management

One Adapter LSM per stack

Each stack's Adapter LSM agrees Active, L1/L2, LinkReset and Disabled with the remote Adapter over sideband. It sits between the RDI state machine below and, for CXL, the ARB/MUX vLSMs above.

Spec§3.5, p.89–90 · §3.6, p.90–91

Negotiation

Agree protocol and flit format at bring-up

In Stage 3 of link initialization the two Adapters exchange capability messages and settle on protocol (PCIe, CXL, Streaming, Management Transport), flit format, Retry and stack configuration. The result is fixed until the PHY goes back to Reset.

Spec§3.2.1, p.62–70 · §3.4, p.85

Where the Adapter is "upper" and "lower"

On FDI the Adapter is the lower layer: it drives pl_*, answers state requests and stalls the Protocol Layer. On RDI it is the upper layer: it drives lp_* and requests states from the PHY. Many Adapter bugs come from mixing those two roles up.

Spec§10.3, p.559

What the Adapter does not do

It does not decode TLPs or CXL messages, train lanes, or pick the link speed. Speed is negotiated by the PHY in Stage 2 (MBINIT.PARAM); the Adapter only reads the result on pl_max_speedmode.

3 · Stage 3: parameter exchange

Starts when RDI reaches Active, ends when FDI reaches Active. Three parts: determine local capabilities, exchange them with the remote Adapter, bring up FDI.

1

Local capabilities. Read PHY results (pl_max_speedmode, pl_lnk_cfg), decide whether Retry is needed, gather Link Control register settings. A Retimer also works out its receive-buffer credits (256B each).

2

DP advertises. For PCIe or CXL, the Downstream Port sends {AdvCap.Adapter} first.

3

UP answers. The Upstream Port waits for it, reviews it, may adjust its own bits, then sends its {AdvCap.Adapter}.

4

DP finalizes. DP sends {FinCap.Adapter} with the agreed bits.

5

CXL details. If 68B Flit Mode or CXL 256B Flit Mode is set in FinCap.Adapter, a second round of {AdvCap.CXL} and DP's {FinCap.CXL} picks CXL vs PCIe and the CXL options.

6

FDI bring-up. The Adapter reports protocol and flit format on FDI, raises pl_inband_pres, and runs the FDI Active handshake per stack. Then the Sequence Number Handshake.

Streaming and Management Transport are simpler

No DP/UP roles and no {FinCap.*}: each side sends its own {AdvCap.Adapter}, and the result is the logical AND of what was sent and received. Vendor-defined messages may negotiate extensions.

Spec§3.2.1.2, p.66–67 · §3.4, p.87

Two different protocols (Enhanced Multi-Protocol)

Stack 0 is negotiated with {AdvCap/FinCap.Adapter}; Stack 1 with {MultiProtAdvCap/MultiProtFinCap.Adapter}, with the same DP-first rule. CXL details for Stack 1 use {*.CXL} with MsgInfo 0001h.

Spec§3.2.1.2, p.67

Timeout: 8 ms (−0% / +50%)

Covers Parts 1 and 2. Counts only while RDI is Active. Resets on any *.Stall message. Expiry is an Uncorrectable Internal Error and the Adapter takes RDI to LinkError. A Retimer resolving with its far partner sends a Stall every 4 ms.

5 · CRC and Retry

One CRC definition for every format; one Retry scheme borrowed from PCIe Flit Mode and simplified for a short, clean link.

CRC-16, always over 128 bytes
  • Polynomial (x+1)(x15+x+1) = x16+x15+x2+1. Guaranteed to detect any 3 random bit errors.
  • Message is always 128B; shorter messages are zero-extended at the MSB end. Reserved bits count as 0.
  • Initial value 0000h. Bit 0 of byte 0 first.
  • Adapter-inserted bytes (header, DLP) are included with the values that will go on the link.
  • crc_gen.vs, attached to the spec PDF, is the golden reference.
  • CRC is sent even when Retry is not negotiated; then a CRC error should be treated as an Uncorrectable Internal Error.

When is Retry required? Whenever the raw BER is worse than 1e-27, unless Raw Format is the only format. 7 · Link states and power management

The Adapter LSM uses the same state set and request/status contract as FDI. PM is entered top-down and exited bottom-up.

1

Protocol Layer asks on FDI, based on its own idle-time algorithm. CXL vLSMs negotiate PM with ALMPs on mainband, through the Retry buffer. PCIe in UCIe Flit Mode does not use PM DLLPs.

2

Adapter LSM negotiates PM with the remote Adapter on sideband, once the vLSMs (if CXL) are in PM and the Retry buffer is empty. Each stack does this independently.

3

RDI: when every Adapter LSM is in PM, the Adapter requests PM on RDI.

4

PHY goes to a deeper power state. The sideband stays on: PM exit starts there.

Exit is the reverse

Active request travels down FDI and RDI; the PHY wakes and retrains via sideband; RDI goes Active on both dies; Adapter LSMs exit PM via sideband; for CXL, ALMPs then bring the vLSMs to Active.

Spec§3.6, p.91

Protocols that don't want PM

PM is mandatory for PCIe and CXL. Other protocols may disable it by always answering a remote PM request with PMNAK. The PHY may map L1 and L2 to one internal state.

Spec§3.6, p.90

Adapter LSM messages

{LinkMgmt.Adapter0/1.Req/Rsp.*} cover Active, L1, L2, LinkReset and Disable (plus Rsp.PMNAK). There is no Adapter-level Retrain or LinkError message: those states always come up from RDI.

8 · Runtime Link Testing with parity

An optional way to measure link health while traffic runs: the Adapter periodically inserts parity bytes that the receiver checks and logs.

  • Insert 64·N bytes after every 256·256·N bytes of data. N comes from "Number of 64 Byte Inserts". Software must use N = 4 in Flit mode, so inserts are 256B.
  • Only bit 0 of each inserted byte carries parity: parity byte X = XOR of all bits of data bytes X, X+64N, X+128N, … within the window. Bits 7:1 are reserved.
  • All transmitted bytes count, including NOP flits and, for 68B, PDS headers and padding. Parity bytes themselves do not count.
  • Counters reset on leaving Active; enabling must be renegotiated before the next Active.
  • Enabled per direction (Tx Enable at the sender, Rx Enable at the receiver), and takes effect only after a Retrain.
  • Retimers neither buffer nor forward parity bytes; parity checks only the local UCIe link.

Spec§3.9, p.95–96

1

Software sets Tx Enable (sender) and Rx Enable (receiver), then triggers a Retrain.

2

3

4

10 · Why each rule exists, with real-time examples

Each card gives an Adapter rule, the reason behind it, a worked example, and what breaks without it. Unless a card says otherwise, numbers assume one x64 Advanced module at 16 GT/s: 128 GB/s per direction, one 256B flit every 2 ns.

Negotiation§3.2.1.2 p.65

The Downstream Port speaks first and decides

For PCIe or CXL, the UP waits for the DP's {AdvCap.Adapter}, may change its own bits after reading it, then answers. Only the DP sends {FinCap.Adapter} and {FinCap.CXL}.

Why

If both sides computed the result independently, any difference in their resolution logic would leave them running different formats. One decider removes that risk. It mirrors PCIe, where the downstream port controls link configuration.

Real-time example

A CPU (DP) supports CXL 256B with Format 6; an accelerator (UP) supports both CXL and PCIe. The UP sees the DP's advert, keeps CXL, and answers. The DP's FinCap.Adapter sets CXL 256B Flit Mode and CXL_LatOpt_Fmt6, so the link runs Format 6.

If you break it

A UP that sends its AdvCap at once, without waiting, may advertise a combination the DP cannot accept, and the DP's FinCap then contradicts what the UP already configured.

Check it in the spec

NegotiationTable 3-1 p.63 · §3.3.2 p.71

68B formats stop at 32 GT/s

68B Flit Mode is mandatory for CXL and PCIe when the negotiated maximum data rate is ≤ 32 GT/s, and must be 0 above it. The Adapter learns the rate from pl_max_speedmode, because it was settled in Stage 2.

Why

CXL 68B mode and PCIe Non-Flit mode only exist up to 32 GT/s in their base specifications; faster PCIe and CXL generations require 256B flits. UCIe follows the protocol rules. The Adapter cannot know the rate on its own, so RDI hands it over before Stage 3 begins.

Real-time example

A Standard Package link negotiates a 48 GT/s maximum in MBINIT.PARAM. pl_max_speedmode = 1, so the Adapter clears 68B Flit Mode and the link must use a 256B format, even if it later degrades to 16 GT/s.

If you break it

An Adapter that advertises 68B at 48 GT/s may settle on Format 2, which is not supported there. Either side may take the link to LinkError on an illegal combination.

Check it in the spec

Negotiation§3.2.1.2 p.70

8 ms to negotiate, but only while RDI is Active

Parameter exchange must finish within 8 ms (−0%/+50%). The timer counts only while RDI is Active and restarts on any Stall message. Expiry is an Uncorrectable Internal Error and RDI goes to LinkError.

Why

A partner that never answers must not hang the link forever. But time spent retraining is not the partner's fault, so the timer pauses when RDI is not Active. Stall messages let a Retimer, which must first resolve capabilities with the far side, buy time.

Real-time example

Host → Retimer → off-package link → Retimer → device. The near Retimer cannot answer until the far side has answered, so it sends {AdvCap.Adapter.Stall} every 4 ms, resetting the host's timer each time.

If you break it

A Retimer that stalls every 10 ms lets the host time out at 8 ms; the link drops to LinkError on every boot.

Check it in the spec
  • SpecTimer, tolerance, reset on Stall, UIE, Retimer 4 ms §3.2.1.2, p.70
  • SpecStall MsgInfo FFFFh Table 7-8, p.276 · p.283
Negotiation§3.4 p.85 · §3.8 p.93

Protocol, format and Retry are fixed until PHY Reset

Once negotiated, protocol and flit format cannot change until the PHY returns to Reset. Retry cannot be turned off even if the link later degrades to a speed that would not need it.

Why

Changing format would change byte positions, CRC coverage and buffer sizes with traffic in flight. A speed degrade happens in the middle of operation (through Retrain), and the Retry state (sequence numbers, buffered flits) would have to be torn down safely. Keeping it on is simpler and costs little at a lower BER.

Real-time example

An Advanced link trains at 32 GT/s with Retry. A runtime Retrain degrades it to 12 GT/s, where BER is 1e-27. Retry stays on; it just never has to replay.

If you break it

An Adapter that drops Retry after degrade disagrees with the remote Adapter about whether flit headers carry sequence numbers.

Check it in the spec
  • SpecFixed until Reset §3.4, p.85
  • SpecRetry not disabled on degrade; renegotiated at next init §3.8, p.93
  • SpecBER by speed Table 1-2, p.40
Flit formats§3.3.3 p.76

The Protocol Layer leaves the Adapter's bytes at zero

In 256B formats the Protocol Layer drives 0 on header and CRC bytes, but fills the Protocol ID bits (and for PCIe/CXL.io, the Flit_Marker bit and fields). On receive, the Adapter passes the flit up unchanged and the Protocol Layer ignores bits that are not its own.

Why

Sequence numbers, Ack/Nak and CRC depend on retry state that only the Adapter has. The protocol knows which protocol a flit belongs to and whether it carries a Flit Marker. Splitting the header along that line means no extra wires on FDI and no rewriting of protocol bytes.

Real-time example

A CXL.cachemem flit arrives on FDI with byte 0 = 10xx_xxxx (Protocol ID 10b) and byte 1 = 0. The Adapter sets the stack bit, the sequence or Ack/Nak fields, then computes CRC over the final bytes.

If you break it

A Protocol Layer that leaves garbage in the CRC bytes is harmless (the Adapter overwrites them). One that sets Protocol ID 00b on a Streaming flit makes the receiver drop it as a NOP.

Check it in the spec
  • SpecDriving 0, Protocol ID, Flit_Marker §3.3.3, p.76 · Table 3-6, p.84
  • SpecStreaming never sets 00b §3.3.3, p.76 · Table 3-5, p.80
Flit formats§3.3.3 p.76

DLLPs bypass Retry, so the DLP bytes are split

For PCIe and CXL.io, DLP bytes 0–1 become the UCIe Flit Header. The Adapter inserts DLLPs from FDI's DLLP interface into DLP bytes 2–5 (as Optimized_Update_FC when possible), unless the flit carries a Flit Marker. On receive, it extracts them back onto FDI.

Why

DLLPs (credit updates, Acks in PCIe) must not be replayed from an old flit: a replayed credit update could double-count credits. Inserting them as the flit leaves, after the retry buffer, gives each transmission (and each replay) the current DLLP.

Real-time example

Flit #57 is replayed 300 ns after its first transmission. The first copy carried an UpdateFC for 8 credits; the replay carries whatever DLLP is current now, not the stale one.

If you break it

Storing DLLPs in the retry buffer: a replay delivers the old UpdateFC again and the receiver's credit counter drifts.

Check it in the spec
  • SpecDLP split, insertion, Optimized_Update_FC, extraction §3.3.3, p.76
  • SpecFDI DLLP side channel Table 10-3, p.540–541 · §10.2.4.1, p.550

Flit formats§3.3.4 p.80–81

Latency-Optimized: each 128B half has its own CRC

Formats 5 and 6 put CRC0 at bytes 126–127 covering bytes 0–125, and CRC1 at 254–255 covering 128–253. Format 6 also lets the Protocol Layer use bytes that Format 5 leaves reserved.

Why

With one CRC at the end of 256B, the receiver must wait for the whole flit before trusting any of it. With a CRC per half, the first 128B can be checked and consumed one half-flit time earlier. That is why the spec strongly recommends Format 6 for CXL 256B.

Real-time example

x64 at 16 GT/s: a 256B flit takes 2 ns on the wire. A CXL.cachemem read response in the first half can be released about 1 ns earlier than with Format 4.

If you break it

A receiver that consumed half 0 and then sees CRC1 fail must not consume half 0 again on replay. The FDI pl_flit_cancel rules for Latency-Optimized flits exist for this case.

Check it in the spec
  • SpecCRC coverage §3.3.4, p.81
  • SpecFormat 6 strongly recommended §3.3.4, p.80 · Table 3-7, p.85
  • DerivedHalf of 2 ns ≈ 1 ns
Flit formats§3.3.2.1 p.73

68B flits: shift by 4 every flit, close with PDS

Each 68B flit adds 4 bytes to a 64B payload, so successive flits shift 4 bytes across the bus. Data on RDI is always a multiple of 256B. To pause, the Adapter sends a PDS header, zeros to the next 64B boundary, at least two more all-zero 64B chunks, and more zeros to reach a 256B multiple.

Why

The PHY and any Retimer work in 256B units (Retimer credits are 256B). The two zero chunks give the receiver's byte shifter time to reset, so the next flit can start at byte 0 of a 256B-aligned transfer.

Real-time example

Three flits then idle: flits use bytes 0–203, PDS header 204–205, zeros 206–255, two zero chunks 256–383, more zeros 384–511. The pause costs 512 bytes total: 4 ns on a x64 16 GT/s link.

If you break it

Stopping right after the PDS header leaves the receiver's shifter mid-flit; the next flit is decoded 4·k bytes off and fails CRC.

Check it in the spec
  • SpecShift, 256B multiples, PDS padding §3.3.2.1, p.73 · Figures 3-12 and 3-13, p.75
  • DerivedThe 3-flit byte positions and 4 ns
Flit formats§3.3.2.1 p.73–74

PDS detection: 2 of 4 with Retry, exact without

A PDS header sets four things: Byte 0 bit 4, Byte 1 bits 7 and 6, and Byte 1 [5:4] = 00b with the expected sequence value. With Retry, the receiver treats a header as PDS if any two hold. Without Retry, conditions 1 and 2 must hold.

Why

At BER 1e-15 a bit flip in the header is possible. "2 of 4" means three bit errors are needed to turn a PDS into a regular header, and even that is caught by CRC and replayed. The reverse (a regular header aliasing to PDS with two errors) is why the spec recommends checking that the first flit after a weak PDS has the expected explicit sequence number.

Real-time example

A PDS arrives with Byte 1 bit 6 flipped. Three of four conditions hold, so it is still a PDS. The receiver then checks that the next valid flit carries the expected sequence number before trusting the stream.

If you break it

Requiring all four with Retry enabled turns a single bit error into a lost PDS, a misaligned stream and a burst of CRC errors.

Check it in the spec
  • SpecConditions and rules §3.3.2.1, p.73
  • SpecAliasing analysis and recommended check Implementation Note, p.74
Flit formats§3.3.2.1 p.73–74

PDS before replay, before Retrain, and how to resume

In 68B format a PDS must be sent before replaying from the retry buffer and before acking a stall for Retrain, so replayed flits start 256B-aligned. With Retry disabled, the first flit after a PDS must be a payload or test flit, not a NOP.

Why

A replay restarts the stream from an older flit, so the byte shifter must restart too. A NOP can be all zeros, which is indistinguishable from PDS padding; without sequence numbers the receiver could not tell that the stream had resumed.

Real-time example

Nak for flit #33 arrives. The Adapter finishes the current flit, sends PDS plus padding, then replays #33 starting at byte 0 of a new 256B block with an explicit sequence number.

If you break it

Replaying without a PDS leaves the remote shifter at the old offset; every replayed flit fails CRC and the replay counter escalates to Retrain.

Check it in the spec
  • SpecPDS on Retry and Retrain; resume rules §3.3.2.1, p.73–74
  • SpecPDS error may map to Retrain (Retry) or LinkError (no Retry) p.74
Flit formatsTable 3-10 notes p.88

Format priority: 6 › 4 › 3 › 2 › 5

When several formats are possible (Truth Table 2), Raw wins if advertised, then Format 6, then Format 4 over 3, then 68B, with Format 5 last.

Why

The spec's notes give the reasons. Raw is only advertised when it is the required mode. Format 6 has the best performance. Start Header (4) beats End Header (3) because the receiver learns the protocol and stack from the first bytes, so multiplexed stacks can be steered earlier. Format 5 carries the most overhead.

Real-time example

Two Streaming stacks; both sides advertise Std End Header, Std Start Header and LatOpt without optional bytes. Result: Format 4.

If you break it

An Adapter that resolves by its own preference ("lowest latency = 5") picks a different format than its partner; traffic is undecodable.

Check it in the spec
  • SpecTruth Tables 1 and 2 with notes a and b Tables 3-9 and 3-10, p.88
CRC & Retry§3.7 p.92

One CRC-16 over 128 bytes, everywhere

CRC polynomial x16+x15+x2+1 over a 128B message, zero-extended at the MSB end, initial value 0, bit 0 of byte 0 first. A 256B flit carries two CRCs; a 68B flit carries one over 66B.

Why

The (x15+x+1) primitive factor guarantees detection of any two bit errors; the (x+1) factor adds odd parity, catching any odd number, so any three are caught. One engine sized for 128B serves every format, which keeps CRC logic small and identical on both dies.

Real-time example

A 68B flit's CRC covers bytes 0–65 (header plus 64B payload). The CRC engine sees bytes 66–127 as zeros. A Format 3 flit's CRC1 covers bytes 128–241 as message bytes 0–113.

If you break it

Computing CRC before inserting the sequence number into the header: every flit fails CRC at the receiver. The spec requires the final transmitted values to be used.

Check it in the spec
  • SpecPolynomial, guarantee, zero extension, init, bit order, golden crc_gen.vs §3.7, p.92
  • Spec68B CRC message mapping §3.3.2, p.71
CRC & Retry§3.8 p.93

Alternate explicit-sequence and Ack/Nak flits

Flit headers carry either the flit's own sequence number or an Ack/Nak, never both. UCIe alternates them (PCIe sends three explicit ones first). Consecutive explicit-sequence flits are allowed only when no Ack/Nak is pending.

Why

Every other flit returns an Ack, so the sender learns quickly what arrived and can free retry-buffer space sooner. Faster Acks mean a smaller retry buffer for the same bandwidth.

Real-time example

With alternation, an Ack can leave every 4 ns (two flits at 2 ns). If the round trip for an Ack is 40 ns, about 20 flits are in flight: 5 KB of retry buffer at 256B per flit.

If you break it

Following PCIe's 3-in-a-row rule triples the worst-case Ack gap and stalls a minimum-sized retry buffer.

Check it in the spec
  • SpecAlternation and CONSECUTIVE_TX_EXPLICIT_SEQ_NUM_FLIT < 1 §3.8, p.93
  • IllustrativeThe 40 ns round trip
  • Derived40 ns ÷ 2 ns × 256B ≈ 5 KB
CRC & Retry§3.8 p.93

No selective Nak, no receive retry buffer

After a bad flit, the receiver drops everything until the sender replays from the Nak'ed flit. Selective Nak and Rx retry-buffer rules from PCIe must not be implemented.

Why

Errors are rare on a 2 mm Advanced Package channel. Go-back-N replay costs a few flits of bandwidth per error; selective repeat would need a reorder buffer on every receiver. The trade-off favours the simpler design.

Real-time example

At BER 1e-15 and 1.024 Tb/s per direction, a bit error arrives about once every 16 minutes. Replaying 5 flits costs 10 ns each time. At 64 GT/s with BER 1e-12 it is about 4 per second, still negligible.

If you break it

A receiver that keeps good flits after a bad one and later accepts the replay as well delivers duplicates.

Check it in the spec
  • SpecSelective Nak and Rx buffer not allowed §3.8, p.93
  • SpecBER values Tables 1-1 and 1-2, p.40
  • Derived1.024e12 × 1e-15 ≈ 1e-3 errors/s; 4.096e12 × 1e-12 ≈ 4/s
CRC & Retry§3.8 p.93

The replay timer counts idle time too

REPLAY_TIMEOUT_FLIT_COUNT is a 9-bit counter that also increments for every Flit Time (lclk cycles to move 256B at the current speed and width) in which no flit was sent. A replay is scheduled at ≥ 375. Timeouts are logged as Correctable Internal Errors.

Why

PCIe sends IDLE flits continuously, so counting flits measures time. UCIe lets the Adapter stop sending NOPs to save power, so a pure flit count could freeze with an unacknowledged flit in the buffer. Counting idle Flit Times restores the link between count and time.

Real-time example

Last payload #88 is sent and its Ack is corrupted. The link goes idle. After 375 Flit Times (750 ns at 2 ns per flit) the sender replays #88 and the remote resends the Ack.

If you break it

Counting only transmitted flits: the idle link never times out, #88 sits unacknowledged forever, and the retry buffer never drains before PM entry.

Check it in the spec
  • Spec9-bit, saturates at 1FFh, Flit Time rule, ≥ 375, logging §3.8, p.93
  • Derived375 × 2 ns = 750 ns
CRC & Retry§3.8 p.94

Sequence Number Handshake on every FDI Active entry

Every time FDI enters Active (bring-up, Retrain exit, PM exit) the Adapters run the PCIe Sequence Number Handshake, using NOP flits if there is no payload. Not done within 128 flits: Retrain. Retry variables reset whenever RDI is in Reset or Retrain.

Why

PCIe uses an IDLE flit handshake when the data link comes up. UCIe already agrees "link up" over sideband, so the IDLE phase is dropped, but both sides still have to agree where the sequence numbers stand before trusting Acks.

Real-time example

After L1 exit, FDI goes Active with no traffic pending. The Adapter sends NOP flits with explicit sequence numbers until the handshake completes, a few dozen ns.

If you break it

Skipping the handshake after Retrain: the first Ack refers to a number from before the Retrain and purges flits that were never received.

Check it in the spec
  • SpecEvery FDI Active entry, 128 flits, NOP use, variable init §3.8, p.93–94
  • Illustrative"A few dozen ns"
CRC & Retry§3.8 Impl. Note p.94

After a Nak, send two Acks before going quiet

Recommended: after handling a received Nak (including any replay), send at least two flits carrying an Ack before stopping transmission. Re-sending the last Ack on a NOP is allowed.

Why

If the only Ack before going idle is corrupted, the partner waits for its replay timer. One extra Ack makes that very unlikely. It is a cheap guard for a link that is allowed to stop sending NOPs.

Real-time example

Die 0 sends its last NOP with Ack 52 and it is corrupted. Without a second Ack, Die 1 replays #52 after 750 ns. With a second Ack on another NOP, Die 1 frees #52 straight away.

If you break it

Nothing fails functionally (replay timeout recovers it), but idle-time latency spikes and PM entry is delayed by stuck retry buffers.

Check it in the spec
  • SpecRecommendation and scenario §3.8 Implementation Note, p.94
CRC & Retry§3.8 p.94

Protocol errors become UIE; "Recovery" becomes Retrain

An invalid sequence number in a received Ack or Nak, or a payload flit with explicit sequence number 0, is an Uncorrectable Internal Error. PCIe conditions that would send the port to Recovery make the Adapter request Retrain on RDI.

Why

UCIe has no LTSSM Recovery: link retraining belongs to the PHY's LTSM, and the Adapter's way to ask for it is a Retrain request on RDI. Sequence corruption that CRC did not catch means the retry state can no longer be trusted, so it escalates.

Real-time example

FLIT_REPLAY_NUM shows the same flit replayed repeatedly without progress. In PCIe the port would enter Recovery; the UCIe Adapter drives lp_state_req = Retrain.

If you break it

An Adapter that logs "Data Link Protocol Error" (a PCIe AER error) instead of UIE reports the wrong error to software.

Check it in the spec
  • SpecUIE mapping; Recovery → Retrain; FLIT_REPLAY_NUM guidance §3.8, p.93–94
  • SpecAdapter Retrain triggers on RDI §10.3.3.4, p.566
Stack mux§3.1 p.59

Never two flits in a row from one stack

With Multi_Protocol_Enable, the Adapter never sends consecutive flits from the same stack: not from FDI, not from the retry buffer, not when restarting after a pause. It may insert NOP flits to keep the rule.

Why

The mode exists so two stacks each designed for half the link rate can share a fast link. Their receive paths can absorb consecutive chunks of one flit at full speed, but not consecutive flits. The rule guarantees each receiver at most half the flit rate.

Real-time example

Two CXL stacks each sized for 32 GT/s CXL SERDES bandwidth share a UCIe link that carries twice that. Only stack 0 is busy: the link sends S0, NOP, S0, NOP… and stack 0 still gets its full designed rate.

If you break it

A replay of five stack-0 flits sent back to back overruns stack 0's receiver, which was never built to accept them at link rate.

Check it in the spec
  • SpecThe rule, NOP insertion, replay case, no fixed pattern §3.1, p.59
  • SpecMotivation: CXL 68B at 32 GT/s SERDES bandwidth §3.1 Implementation Note, p.59
Stack mux§3.1 p.59–60

NOP flits: zeros that never enter the retry buffer

A NOP flit has the Adapter's Protocol ID (00b) in its header and an all-zero body. It may bypass the Tx Retry buffer, and the receiving Adapter never forwards it to a Protocol Layer.

Why

NOPs carry no data, so there is nothing to replay. Keeping them out of the retry buffer saves space and lets the Adapter insert them freely, even during a replay.

Real-time example

A replay of #20–#24 (all stack 0) becomes #20, NOP, #21, NOP, … The NOPs are generated on the fly, not stored.

If you break it

Storing NOPs in the retry buffer and giving them sequence numbers wastes buffer space and may push MAX_UNACKNOWLEDGED_FLITS to its limit.

Check it in the spec
  • SpecNOP definition and handling §3.1, p.59
  • SpecProtocol ID 00b Table 3-5, p.80
Stack mux§3.1 p.60

Enhanced mode: receivers advertise a limit, round robin if both are unlimited

With Enhanced Multi_Protocol_Enable, each Adapter advertises per stack whether its receiver takes 100% or only 50% of the link. The transmitter must support both. If both stacks may use 100%, arbitration is per flit and round robin.

Why

Different protocols on the two stacks may have very different receive capacity. Advertising a per-stack limit lets a fast stack use the full link while a slow stack is protected. Round robin stops one busy stack from starving the other.

Real-time example

Stack 0 PCIe (full-rate receiver), stack 1 Streaming (receiver limited to 50%). Stack 0 can send back-to-back flits; stack 1 flits are always separated by another flit or a NOP.

If you break it

A fixed-priority arbiter favouring stack 0 starves stack 1 under load, violating the round-robin requirement.

Check it in the spec
  • Spec100%/50%, round robin, per flit §3.1, p.60
  • SpecStack 0/1 Maximum Bandwidth_Limit bits Table 3-1, p.65
Stack mux§3.1 p.59

Multiplexing and Raw Format are mutually exclusive

Neither Multi_Protocol_Enable nor Enhanced Multi_Protocol_Enable can be used with Raw Format.

Why

Demultiplexing relies on the stack ID bit in the flit header, and Raw Format has no header. It also has no NOP flit, so the alternation rule could not be kept.

Real-time example

A design with two Streaming stacks that wants Raw Format must give each its own UCIe link.

If you break it

Advertising both is a configuration error; the negotiated combination is invalid.

  • SpecMutual exclusion §3.1, p.59–60

States & PM§3.6 p.90–91

PM entry top-down: vLSM, Adapter LSM, RDI

For CXL, both vLSMs reach PM via ALMPs first, then the retry buffer must be empty, then the Adapter LSM negotiates PM on sideband, and only when all Adapter LSMs are in PM does RDI go to PM.

Why

Each layer must be idle before the layer below can turn off. ALMPs ride through the retry buffer, so the buffer must be drained (all ALMPs acknowledged) before the mainband is allowed to stop.

Real-time example

CXL.io idles first, CXL.cachemem 3 µs later. Only when both vLSMs report L1 and the last ALMP is acked does the Adapter send {LinkMgmt.Adapter0.Req.L1}.

If you break it

Entering Adapter-LSM PM with an unacknowledged ALMP in the retry buffer: the replay timer fires after the PHY has powered down.

Check it in the spec

  • SpecCXL 68B over UCIe uses 256B ALMP formats and flows §3.6, p.90–91
States & PM§3.5 p.89–90 · Table 7-8

Adapter LSMs never negotiate Retrain or LinkError

There are no {LinkMgmt.Adapter*} messages for Retrain or LinkError. RDI goes to Retrain or LinkError first and pushes it up to the Adapter LSMs. LinkReset and Disabled go the other way: negotiated per Adapter LSM, and pushed down to RDI only when every stack is there.

Why

Retrain and LinkError are about the physical link, shared by both stacks, so the PHY owns them. LinkReset and Disabled are about one protocol's reset semantics, so they belong to that stack and must not disturb the other.

Real-time example

Stack 0 (PCIe) hot-resets: its Adapter LSM goes to LinkReset via {LinkMgmt.Adapter0.Req.LinkReset}. Stack 1 keeps running. A framing error then retrains RDI, and both Adapter LSMs that are Active see Retrain.

If you break it

An Adapter that takes RDI to LinkReset for one stack's hot reset drops the other stack's traffic.

Check it in the spec
  • SpecHierarchy rules §3.5, p.89–90
  • SpecAdapter0/1 message list Table 7-8, p.276–277
Runtime parity§3.9 p.95 · §4.1.1 p.98

The 64N stride makes it per-lane parity

Parity byte X covers data bytes X, X+64N, X+128N, … over a 256·256·N-byte window. With N = 4 that is 256 parity bytes per 256 KiB of data, each covering 1,024 data bytes.

Why

Bytes are striped across lanes, and on a x64 module byte k of each 64B transfer goes to the same lane every time. Because the stride is a multiple of 64, all bytes covered by one parity bit travelled on the same lane. A parity error therefore points at a lane, which is exactly what you want when deciding whether to repair or degrade.

Real-time example

N = 4 at 128 GB/s: 256B of parity every 262,144B, an insert every 2.05 µs and 0.098% overhead. Repeated parity errors at byte positions X ≡ 17 (mod 64) implicate lane 17.

If you break it

Counting parity bytes themselves as data, or not counting NOPs and PDS padding, puts the receiver's window out of step with the transmitter's; every check fails.

Check it in the spec
  • SpecInsert rate, N = 4, parity formula, what counts §3.9, p.95–96
  • SpecByte-to-lane mapping §4.1.1, p.98
  • DerivedPer-lane property, 1,024 bytes per parity bit, 2.05 µs, 0.098%
Runtime parity§3.9 p.95–96

Enable in Retrain, with an explicit Ack

Parity is enabled per direction and takes effect only after a Retrain. During Retrain (or L1) the sending Adapter sends {ParityFeature.Req}; the receiver Acks or Naks. The requester must not exit Retrain or L1 on RDI until it has the answer.

Why

The receiver may need to switch to a slower data path to strip and check parity bytes. If the transmitter started inserting before the receiver was ready, parity bytes would be decoded as flits. The spec says the explicit handshake exists to give both sides time to switch.

Real-time example

Software enables Tx on Die 1 and Rx on Die 0, then sets the Retrain bit. In Retrain, Die 1 sends ParityFeature.Req; Die 0 answers Ack; Die 1 only then gives the NOP→Active edge on RDI.

If you break it

A requester that exits Retrain before the Ack: Die 0's Rx path is still in normal mode when parity bytes arrive.

Check it in the spec
  • SpecNegotiation, ordering, logging, timeout §3.9, p.95–96 · Figures 3-30, 3-31
  • SpecNOP→Active edge lets the Adapter wait §10.3.3.4 note, p.567

14 · Test yourself

Interview-style questions, basic to advanced.

Name the four jobs of the D2D Adapter. Basic

Reliable data transfer (CRC and Retry, or parity), arbitration and multiplexing of protocol stacks, link state management, and protocol/parameter negotiation with the remote partner.

Spec§3.0, p.58

In Format 2, what does the Adapter add to the Protocol Layer's 64 bytes? Basic

A 2-byte Flit Header in front and a 2-byte CRC behind, computed over the header and payload (66 bytes, zero-extended to 128). Then it shifts the result across the RDI bus, since each flit is 4 bytes longer than 64.

Spec§3.3.2, p.71 · §3.3.2.1, p.73

Which format is mandatory for PCIe Flit Mode, and which for CXL 256B Flit Mode? Basic

PCIe Flit Mode: Format 3, Standard 256B End Header. CXL 256B Flit Mode: Format 4, Standard 256B Start Header. Format 6 is strongly recommended for both (for PCIe, if the capability is supported).

SpecTable 3-7, p.85

Who sends {FinCap.Adapter}, and why not both sides? Intermediate

Only the Downstream Port, after the Upstream Port has replied to its advertisement. One decider means both dies always end up with the same configuration. For Streaming there is no FinCap at all; each side ANDs what it sent and received.

Spec§3.2.1.2, p.65–67

Why does UCIe alternate explicit-sequence flits with Ack/Nak flits? Intermediate

To return Acks faster, which shortens how long flits stay in the retry buffer and lets it be smaller. Consecutive explicit-sequence flits are allowed only when no Ack/Nak is pending.

Spec§3.8, p.93

Why does REPLAY_TIMEOUT_FLIT_COUNT also count idle Flit Times? Intermediate

Because UCIe lets a transmitter stop sending NOP flits when idle, to save power. A count of transmitted flits would freeze, and an unacknowledged flit would never be replayed. Counting idle Flit Times keeps the counter tied to time; replay is scheduled at ≥ 375.

Spec§3.8, p.93

What is a PDS and when must the Adapter send one? Intermediate

Pause of Data Stream, in 68B format: a special 2-byte header followed by zeros to the next 64B boundary, two more zero 64B chunks, and zeros up to a 256B multiple. Sent before pausing an idle stream (unless sending continuous NOPs instead), before replaying, and before acking a stall for Retrain.

Spec§3.3.2.1, p.73

With Multi_Protocol_Enable, only stack 0 has traffic. What does the link carry? Advanced

Stack-0 flits separated by NOP flits (or stack-1 flits when there are any): never two stack-0 flits in a row. NOPs bypass the retry buffer and are dropped by the receiving Adapter. The same applies during replay.

Spec§3.1, p.59

Why is there no {LinkMgmt.Adapter0.Req.Retrain}? Advanced

Retrain (and LinkError) concern the shared physical link, so they start at RDI and are pushed up to every Active Adapter LSM. The Adapter asks for a retrain by requesting Retrain on RDI. LinkReset and Disabled, by contrast, are per stack and are negotiated per Adapter LSM.

Spec§3.5, p.89–90 · Table 7-8 · §10.3.3.4

Why does runtime parity use a stride of 64·N bytes? Advanced

Every byte covered by one parity bit travels on the same lane, because byte k of each 64B transfer always goes to lane k on a x64 module. So parity errors point to a specific lane. (This is a consequence of §3.9 and §4.1.1, not stated as a reason in the spec.)

Derived§3.9, p.95 · §4.1.1, p.98

A Latency-Optimized flit passes CRC0 and fails CRC1. What happens? Advanced

Half 0 may already have been forwarded and consumed. Half 1 is cancelled on FDI and a Nak triggers replay of the whole flit. When the replay arrives, the receiver must not consume half 0 a second time; the FDI cancel rules cover this.

Spec§3.3.4, p.81 · §10.2.5, p.551

The link degrades from 32 GT/s to 12 GT/s at runtime. Can Retry be turned off? Can the format change? Advanced

No to both. Retry stays enabled until the next link initialization (RDI to Reset), and protocol and flit format are fixed until the PHY goes to Reset.
